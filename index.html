About

I am an applied environmental economist completing my PhD at Africa Business School, University Mohammed VI Polytechnic in Morocco, under a joint doctoral agreement with the Programa de Engenharia de Produção at COPPE, Federal University of Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. My doctorate is formally in Management and Organizational Sciences, supervised by Prof. Amine Ben Amar (Finance Cluster, ABS) and Prof. Olga Gorelkina (Moroccan Center for Game Theory), with Prof. Luan Santos (COPPE, UFRJ) as co-supervisor. My dissertation asks who absorbs climate and carbon-pricing risk, and why capacity rather than exposure usually decides: how fossil revenue is absorbed into emissions in producer economies, why states underprice carbon, which exporters can document their emissions to a foreign regulator, and what sovereign credit markets actually price.

My research covers the carbon footprint of fossil-fuel windfalls in producer economies, the decomposition of carbon price deficits across 174 countries, exposure to the EU carbon border adjustment mechanism among African exporters, sovereign CDS dynamics and climate vulnerability, heat and the European food and drink chain, household coping with climate shocks across ten African countries, and corporate bond spreads in emerging markets. I work with shift-share instruments and oil supply news, local projections, SVAR with recursive identification, time-varying local projections with stochastic volatility, wavelet coherence analysis, within-household first differences, and randomisation inference.

Before pursuing doctoral studies, I spent about three years at OCP Africa in Nigeria as a Regional Field Project Manager and Research Agronomist, where I led agricultural development initiatives in partnership with USAID/TradeHub. I also founded AgriBox SARL, a climate-smart hydroponic enterprise in Morocco.

Job Market Paper  ·  Submitted  ·  Environmental and Resource Economics

Where the Windfall Goes: Fossil Revenue, Domestic Absorption and Carbon Emissions

Uke, O. D.

Abstract

Every rise in the world price of oil, gas or coal transfers income to producer economies, and the energy transition will make these transfers more volatile before it makes them smaller. Using national accounts, government revenue and sector-level fossil CO₂ for 163 countries over 1981–2024, and a windfall shock built from predetermined fossil rents and unexpected world fuel prices, I estimate what windfalls do to producer economies and their emissions. In the economies with data on resource revenue, almost all of the windfall reaches the government budget. Most of it is saved abroad in the first year and absorbed into consumption, investment and imports within two years. Each dollar of windfall revenue adds 0.52 kg of CO₂ at home (95 per cent weak-instrument-robust interval: 0.20 to 0.89 kg), about half the carbon intensity of an average dollar of gross domestic product, mostly through road transport, power and buildings. Cement emissions trace an investment channel that runs through existing kilns. Instrumenting the windfall with oil supply news from announcements by the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries gives macroeconomic, construction, buildings and road transport responses of the same size and, in independent energy statistics, an equally large rise in energy CO₂ averaged over years zero to five. In cement, buildings and power, booms raise emissions and busts of the same size do not lower them. Over the 2003–2014 commodity boom, windfalls added about 2.7 billion tonnes of CO₂, with climate damage of roughly 500 billion dollars.

Riding the Climate Wave: A Wavelet Analysis of Sovereign Risk in Developed and Developing Economies

Uke, O. D., Ben Amar, A., Gorelkina, O.

Abstract

We examine which dimension of climate risk sovereign credit markets price: countries’ physical exposure to climate change (vulnerability) or their capacity to adapt to it (readiness). We use five-year sovereign CDS spreads for 59 advanced and emerging market and developing economies (EMDEs) over 2008–2021, along with ND-GAIN vulnerability and readiness indices. Using continuous wavelet coherence analysis, we first document a significant co-movement of climate vulnerability and CDS spreads at short-, medium- and low-frequency bands. This co-movement is neither concentrated in the long horizon nor systematically dependent on the type of economy (advanced vs. EMDE). To separate the effects of exposure and adaptive capacity, we then estimate regressions on country-level means of each variable. We find that while the vulnerability gradient vanishes when the development level is controlled, readiness has a strong negative association with the CDS spread net of income, public debt and the Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI). For EMDEs, closing the readiness disadvantage relative to advanced economies is associated with about 74 per cent smaller spreads. On the policy side, our results imply that rating agencies and climate stress tests should price the adaptive capacity of sovereigns together with their physical exposure, and should not treat climate-related sovereign risk as a concern of low-income issuers or long maturities only.

Fiscal Capacity, Not Climate Exposure: Decomposing Global Fossil-Fuel Underpricing and Its Equity Burden

Uke, O. D., Gorelkina, O., Ben Amar, A.

Abstract

Most of the world prices carbon below its social cost, and most countries price it below zero. We assemble effective carbon prices, the explicit carbon price minus the implicit subsidy per tonne of carbon dioxide, for 174 countries over 2015–2022, benchmark them against three social cost of carbon estimates, and decompose the shortfall into a domestic wedge, whose costs fall on the underpricing country itself through supply-cost gaps, foregone consumption taxes and local air pollution, and a global climate wedge. Underpricing is nearly universal: only Sweden, Norway and Finland price above zero. The two wedges behave differently. Fiscal capacity, measured as non-resource tax revenue, predicts the domestic wedge, which accounts for roughly two thirds of the global total; the association is robust and holds on the purely cash components, where no valuation of externalities is involved. No observable characteristic of states predicts the climate wedge. Climate vulnerability does not raise deficits, but vulnerability and weak fiscal capacity coincide so closely across countries that the income-normalised burden of underpricing is about twenty times higher in the most vulnerable quintile. The findings assign different instruments to the two wedges: domestic revenue mobilisation is climate policy for the larger part; the climate wedge requires international coordination. Replication package.

Financial Reach and Household Coping under Climate Shocks: Evidence from Ten African Countries

Uke, O. D., Cort, T. (Yale School of Management)

Abstract

Financial inclusion in Africa has grown, but many households struggle to finance coping with climate shocks. Standard asset pricing implies that the value of a financial claim depends on the correlation of its underlying income stream with the shock. We therefore define financial adaptation capacity as the amount of support available from financial instruments that are underpinned by income streams less correlated with local climate shocks. Satellite rainfall suggests that growing season rainfall shortfalls are correlated over distances of 250 km. Exploiting newly harmonised shock and coping modules from 10 African countries, we compare, within the same household, coping with a local, covariate climate shock versus an idiosyncratic shock. First, covariate shocks are associated with a 2 percentage point higher probability of coping through consumption reduction relative to idiosyncratic shocks, which are instead associated with asset sales in Malawi. Second, among transfer recipients in West Africa, the difference in the probability of consumption coping after covariate versus idiosyncratic shocks is 4.6 percentage points lower if the transfers originate from elsewhere in the country versus within the region. We find no corresponding pattern for credit. Third, consumption coping is associated with a roughly 10 percentage point higher probability of reporting food shortages. Despite the non-causal nature of the estimates, due to households’ choice of financial instruments and migrant links, we show that the conventional metric of financial inclusion, account ownership, fails to capture the extent to which households are resilient to climate shocks.

Fossil fuel underpricing, climate disasters and the carbon composition of manufacturing: Evidence from global value chains

Uke, O. D.

Abstract

Holding domestic energy prices below their efficient level can shield energy users from cost shocks, yet little is known about whether fossil fuel underpricing shapes how industrial structure adjusts to climate disasters. This paper asks whether, in climate-disaster years, manufacturing value chains shift towards carbon-intensive sectors in economies where domestic energy prices sit furthest below their efficient level. We combine global value chain output for 77 countries and 18 manufacturing sectors over 2016–2022 with IMF estimates of fossil fuel underpricing and EM-DAT disaster records. In disaster years, the share of manufacturing value-chain output produced by carbon-intensive sectors rises with underpricing (p = 0.006), by about one percentage point at the 90th percentile of underpricing, while it falls slightly and insignificantly where fuel is priced close to cost. The pattern holds with country-specific trends and with a control for fossil rent exposure, and sector-level estimates with country-sector, country-year and sector-year fixed effects point the same way (p = 0.041). Sanctions and armed conflict show no comparable pattern, and the association is strongest in resource-rich economies with weak democratic accountability. Because underpricing varies mainly across countries, the estimates compare pricing regimes and are conditional associations. The findings suggest that appraisals of fossil fuel subsidy reform and of disaster relief should consider how pricing regimes shape the sectoral incidence of climate shocks, alongside fiscal savings and emission reductions. Replication package.

Working Paper  ·  October 2026

Heat beyond the farm gate: Offsetting supply and demand responses in European food and drink manufacturing

Uke, O. D., Ben Amar, A., Gorelkina, O.

Abstract

Heat reaches food and drink manufacturers through the supply of farm inputs and the demand for their products, and the two channels can move output in opposite directions. We estimate monthly responses to weather for food and drink industries in 21 European countries, seven of which report food groups, and for raw milk deliveries and slaughter, over 2000–2026. Panel local projections identify the responses from differences between countries’ weather in the same month. In the average country, a summer month 1 °C warmer than normal lowers raw milk deliveries by 0.3% and bakery output by 0.8% and raises beverage output by 2.0%; meat processing falls by 0.6%, an unweighted figure concentrated among the smaller producers. The milk and meat losses follow days above 30 °C rather than heat that is unusual for the season, occur in warm but not in cool months and are not recovered, as heat stress in livestock would produce. Beverage output follows both kinds of heat, falls in cool months as much as it rises in warm ones and moves with the price of non-alcoholic beverages, as a demand shift would. Because the responses offset, 81% of the gross change in value added cancels, the food products total barely moves, and beverages are the only one of 24 manufacturing divisions to respond. The beverage response has halved since the early 2000s and is weaker where hot summers are common. Aggregate food output therefore understates how exposed the livestock-based food chain is to heat.

Carbon Accounting Capability and Exposure to the EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism: Evidence from African Exporters

Uke, O. D., Santos, L. (COPPE, UFRJ)

Abstract

Border carbon pricing is mostly studied as trade policy falling on countries, but the European mechanism charges consignments at a penalty default unless the producer verifies its emissions on the regulatory boundary. The capability it rewards is boundary fit: emissions measured on the system boundary that the regulation prices. Across 1,760 tariff codes, priced with default values and the statutory mark-up, African exporters face EUR 2,187.3 million a year on 2023–2025 trade at full phase-in, up to 243.7 per cent of customs value on cement clinker, while in every sector the most processed goods carry the lowest charge. In five installation cases boundary fit does not follow ownership, size or country income: one verified declaration is 6.7 times its charged default, while another producer’s conversion removes 49.2 per cent of its charge. Explicit carbon-price deductions miss fossil subsidies, hiding 24 to 69 per cent of the required reform.

Climate Shocks and Food Manufacturing: Aggregation Bias in the United Kingdom

Uke, O. D., Ben Amar, A., Gorelkina, O.

Abstract

Presented at the 3rd QFDAM Conference, Rabat, June 2026. Climate-macro studies typically model the food system as a single sector. Using UK quarterly data for 1990–2025, we separate primary agriculture from downstream food manufacturing and ask what a single food-sector climate coefficient conceals. A food-system VARX estimated on the same data has an impact response less than half the agricultural one, and a Monte Carlo experiment shows that it systematically erases the persistent decline the disaggregated estimates imply. A single food-sector climate coefficient therefore depends on sectoral composition and is not a structural parameter. The sectoral response estimates are being rebuilt.

Sovereign Climate Risk and Corporate Bond Spreads: Evidence from Emerging Markets

Uke, O. D., Santos, L. (COPPE, UFRJ)

Abstract

Monthly panel of emerging-market corporate bonds for 2018–2024, matched to sovereign CDS, rating actions, EM-DAT disasters and physically measured tropical cyclone tracks, with pre-registered hypotheses and randomisation inference. Current estimates find no sovereign transmission, no change in market access and no average corporate repricing after disasters, and do not support state backing as an insulation mechanism.
